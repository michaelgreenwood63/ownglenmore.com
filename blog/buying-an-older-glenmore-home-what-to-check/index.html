Buying an Older Glenmore Home: What to Check Before You Offer

September 2026 · By Mark Jontz & Associates · 5 min read

Old Glenmore's biggest draw — mature trees, larger lots, real character homes — comes with a flip side buyers sometimes underweight: some of that housing stock is genuinely old, and "character" can mean anything from "beautifully maintained original" to "needs $80K of deferred work." Here's what we actually check before a client offers on an older Glenmore home.

Electrical: Knob-and-Tube and Aluminum Wiring

Homes built before the 1960s can still have knob-and-tube wiring, and homes from roughly the 1960s-70s sometimes used aluminum wiring — both of which most insurers either refuse to cover or require an inspection and upgrade certificate for. Before you write an offer on a genuinely older Glenmore home, ask directly whether the wiring has been updated, and confirm you can actually get insurance before your subject-removal date, not after.

Plumbing: Galvanized Pipes and Polybutylene

Older galvanized steel plumbing corrodes from the inside and gradually restricts water flow — a home with consistently low water pressure is a common tell. Polybutylene piping, used in some homes from the 1980s-90s, has a known failure history and is worth confirming with your inspector. Neither is necessarily a dealbreaker, but both are real budget items to price into your offer, not surprises to discover at possession.

Roof Age and Foundation

Ask for the roof's actual age, not just "looks fine from the street" — a standard asphalt roof has a defined lifespan, and replacement isn't cheap. On foundation, older Glenmore homes on sloped or mature lots are worth a specific look for settling cracks, drainage issues, or signs of past water intrusion, especially if there's been any landscaping or grading change since the home was built.

Updates vs. Original: Get Specific

A listing that says "updated kitchen" or "renovated" doesn't tell you what's actually original underneath. Ask what specifically was done, when, and by whom — permits pulled or not. A cosmetic refresh (paint, cabinet fronts) and a genuine renovation (new subfloor, updated electrical behind the walls) look identical in photos and are very different in practice.

None of this means avoid older Glenmore homes. The lots, the tree canopy, and the location are genuinely hard to replace, and a well-maintained older home is a great buy. The point is knowing which one you're actually looking at — we build a proper inspection contingency into every offer on an older Glenmore property and walk clients through what the inspector finds line by line.
